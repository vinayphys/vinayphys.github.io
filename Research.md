# Research  

My work focuses on the physics of soft and disordered materials—glassy liquids, amorphous solids, polymers, and dense active matter—using large-scale simulations and theory, often in close connection with experiments.

---

## Dense Active Matter: Jamming and Fluidization

I study how dense active materials—systems of self‑propelled particles—transition between jammed, solid-like states and fluidized, flowing states. In ongoing work on dense athermal active glasses [1–3], I look at how increasing activity (self-propulsion) destabilizes very stable amorphous solids, how preparation history affects the critical activity needed to fluidize, and how collective rearrangements and avalanches emerge near this transition. A central theme is the similarity between activity-induced fluidization and yielding in conventional amorphous solids, and how dynamic heterogeneity appears in both settings.

**Related articles:**  
1. *Jamming–to-Fluidization Transition in Dense Athermal Active Matter* (ongoing)  
2. *Avalanches in dense active matter* (ongoing)  
3. *Dynamic heterogeneity in active liquids* (ongoing)

## Polymer Viscoelasticity from Non-affine Lattice Dynamics

In collaboration with experimental and industrial partners, I work on connecting atomistic descriptions of polymers to their macroscopic mechanical response. Using non‑affine lattice dynamics [1–3], we develop methods that predict polymer viscosity and viscoelastic moduli over many orders of magnitude in frequency, from fast molecular vibrations to slow mechanical tests. This allows one to “bridge” time and length scales and to interpret experimental data (e.g. on epoxy resins and glassy polymers) directly in terms of microscopic structure and dynamics.

## Thermal Response of Glassy Systems

I explore how glasses respond to temperature gradients and local heating, with an eye on both fundamental transport and practical processing. In a binary Lennard‑Jones glass [1], I studied the Soret effect and showed how heat transport and mass transport decouple near the glass transition, leading to strong concentration gradients and nonlinear thermal response. In ongoing work on ultrastable polydisperse glasses [2], I investigate how a locally heated region nucleates a melting front and how this front propagates through the glass, helping to clarify when melting occurs via a moving front versus more homogeneously.

**Related articles:**  
1. V. Vaibhav, J. Horbach, and P. Chaudhuri, *Phys. Rev. E* **101**, 022605 (2020).  
2. V. Vaibhav, J. Horbach, and P. Chaudhuri, *Front propagation during melting of glass via local heating* (in preparation, 2026).

## Rheology and Failure of Glassy and Complex Liquids

I study how complex liquids and amorphous solids flow and fail under shear and pressure-driven conditions. In size‑disperse glass‑forming mixtures [1], I showed how a rigid matrix of large particles can coexist with mobile small particles and how this mixed structure affects bulk rheology and flow curves. In confined Poiseuille flow [2], I examined how stress gradients, boundary conditions, and thermalization protocols alter local flow relative to bulk behavior. More recently, I have focused on deliberately designing spatial inhomogeneities in glasses [3] so that shear bands and failure can be steered to specific regions, linking microscopic stability variations to macroscopic failure pathways.

**Related articles:**

## Supercooled Glassy Liquids and Dynamic Heterogeneity

I investigate how particle motions become intermittent and heterogeneous as liquids are cooled toward the glass transition. In a glass-forming mixture with a large size ratio [1], I analyzed diffusion and showed how apparent transport of slow particles can be dominated by collective motion of the system, and how to correctly extract true localization and glass-transition signatures. Together with collaborators [2–3], I use information‑theoretic measures (negentropy) applied to displacement distributions to define entropic timescales of dynamic heterogeneity and to compare them with traditional measures based on moments of the distribution. This work also explores “Brownian yet non‑Gaussian” diffusion and persistent non‑Gaussian tails in metastable liquids, linking them to rare, extreme rearrangements.
