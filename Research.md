# Research

My research focuses on the physics of soft and disordered materials—including glassy liquids, amorphous solids, polymers, and dense active matter—combining large-scale computer simulations with theoretical frameworks, frequently in close synergy with experiments.

---

## Dense Active Matter: Jamming and Fluidization

I investigate how dense active materials—systems composed of self-propelled entities—transition between jammed, solid-like states and fluidized, flowing states. In ongoing studies on dense athermal active glasses [1–3], I explore how increasing self-propulsion destabilizes highly stable amorphous solids, how thermal or processing history dictates the critical activity threshold required for fluidization, and how collective rearrangements and avalanches develop near this transition. A central theme of this work is establishing parallels between activity-induced fluidization and yielding in conventional amorphous solids, particularly in how dynamic heterogeneity manifests across both regimes.

**Related Publications:**
1. *Jamming-to-Fluidization Transition in Dense Athermal Active Matter* (in preparation, 2026).
2. *Avalanches in Dense Active Matter* (in preparation, 2026).
3. *Dynamic Heterogeneity in Active Liquids* (in preparation, 2026).

## Polymer Viscoelasticity from Non-affine Lattice Dynamics

In collaboration with experimental and industrial partners, I work on bridging microscopic atomistic descriptions of polymers to their macroscopic mechanical response. Utilizing non-affine lattice dynamics [1–3], we develop theoretical and computational approaches to predict polymer viscosity and viscoelastic moduli across many orders of magnitude in frequency—spanning fast molecular vibrations down to slow macroscopic mechanical testing. This framework enables seamless scale-bridging, allowing experimental data (e.g., on epoxy resins and glassy polymers) to be interpreted directly through the lens of microscopic structure and relaxation dynamics.

**Related Publications:**

## Thermal Response of Glassy Systems

I study how glassy materials respond to temperature gradients and localized heating, with implications for both fundamental transport theory and material processing techniques. In a binary Lennard-Jones glass [1], I investigated the Soret effect and demonstrated how heat and mass transport decouple near the glass transition, driving strong concentration gradients and non-linear thermal responses. In ongoing work on ultrastable polydisperse glasses [2], I examine how locally heated regions nucleate a melting front and how this front propagates through the glassy matrix, clarifying the conditions that govern whether melting proceeds via an advancing interface versus homogeneous melting.

**Related Publications:**
1. V. Vaibhav, J. Horbach, and P. Chaudhuri, *Phys. Rev. E* **101**, 022605 (2020).
2. V. Vaibhav, J. Horbach, and P. Chaudhuri, *Front Propagation During Melting of Glass via Local Heating* (in preparation, 2026).

## Rheology and Failure of Glassy and Complex Liquids

I analyze how complex liquids and amorphous solids deform, flow, and fail under shear and pressure-driven flows. In size-polydisperse glass-forming mixtures [1], I identified how a rigid network of larger particles can coexist with mobile smaller particles, and how this dynamic phase separation influences bulk rheology and flow curves. In confined Poiseuille flow [2], I examined how stress gradients, boundary conditions, and thermalization protocols modify local flow behavior relative to bulk response. More recently, my work has focused on engineering deliberate spatial inhomogeneities in glasses [3] to control and direct shear bands and failure pathways, directly linking microscopic stability variations to macroscopic failure modes.

## Supercooled Glassy Liquids and Dynamic Heterogeneity

I investigate how particle motion becomes increasingly intermittent and spatially heterogeneous as liquids are supercooled toward the glass transition. In a glass-forming mixture with large size asymmetry [1], I analyzed diffusion and showed how the apparent transport of slow particles can be dominated by system-wide collective motion, providing a scheme to properly isolate true localization and glass-transition signatures. Together with collaborators [2–3], I employ information-theoretic measures (negentropy) on particle displacement distributions to define entropic timescales of dynamic heterogeneity, comparing them against traditional moment-based metrics. This work also elucidates "Brownian yet non-Gaussian" diffusion and persistent non-Gaussian tails in metastable liquids, connecting them to rare, extreme structural rearrangements.
